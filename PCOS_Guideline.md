Evidence-Based Adolescent PCOS Risk Assessment Information Sheet
Source guideline
This risk assessment framework is based on:
• Peña AS, Witchel SF, Boivin J, Burgert TS, Ee C, Hoeger KM, et al.
International evidence-based recommendations for polycystic ovary
syndrome in adolescents. BMC Medicine. 2025;23:151.
doi:10.1186/s12916-025-03901-w.
• 2023 International Evidence-Based Guideline for the Assessment and
Management of PCOS, adolescent-specific recommendations summarized
in Peña et al. 2025.
1. Diagnostic logic underlying the tool
1.1 Core diagnostic criteria in adolescents
The adolescent guideline states that PCOS diagnosis in adolescents is based on
the presence of both of the following features, after exclusion of other disorders
that mimic PCOS:
1. Irregular menstrual cycles, defined according to years since menarche
(gynecologic age).
2. Clinical and/or biochemical hyperandrogenism.
Adolescents with only one of these features are not diagnosed with PCOS but are
considered “at risk” and require longitudinal follow-up.
Pelvic ultrasound (polycystic ovary morphology, PCOM) and anti-Müllerian
hormone (AMH) should not be used for diagnosing PCOS during adolescence.
Key wording from Peña et al. 2025 (Abstract and Results):
“The diagnosis is based on the presence of both irregular menstrual cycles
(defined according to the time postmenarche) and clinical/biochemical
hyperandrogenism following the exclusion of other disorders that mimic PCOS.
Adolescents with only one of these features can be considered ‘at risk’ of PCOS
requiring the management of symptoms and ongoing follow-up. Polycystic
ovarian morphology on pelvic ultrasonography or anti-Müllerian hormone levels
should not be used for diagnosis during adolescence.”
2. Questionnaire items and guideline justification
2.1 Menstrual history items
Item 1. Age at menarche (“How old were you when your first period
started?”)
• Guideline context: Adolescents are defined as 10–19 years old, and
gynecologic age is years post-menarche. The guideline notes that the mean
age of menarche differs across populations.
• Use in tool: Age at menarche is a contextual variable used to interpret
menstrual irregularity relative to gynecologic age.
Item 2. Cycle length and perceived regularity (“How many days are usually
between your periods?” / “Are your periods usually regular?”)
• Guideline definition of irregular cycles (Table 4, consensus
recommendation):
o Normal in the first year post-menarche as part of the pubertal
transition.
o 1 to <3 years post-menarche: <21 or >45 days.
o ≥3 years post-menarche to perimenopause: <21 or >35 days or <8
cycles per year.
o ≥1 year post-menarche: >90 days for any one cycle.
o Primary amenorrhea by age 15 or >3 years after thelarche.
• Use in tool: These thresholds are used to classify cycles as physiologically
irregular (early puberty) versus pathologically irregular (ongoing ovulatory
dysfunction). Menstrual irregularities that meet these definitions are one
of the two required diagnostic criteria for adolescent PCOS.
Item 3. Long gaps between periods (“Do you sometimes go more than 90
days without a period?”)
• Guideline reference: Irregular menstrual cycles include “1 year post
menarche >90 days for any one cycle,” which is used as a criterion for
ovulatory dysfunction.
• Use in tool: Persistent cycles >90 days ≥1 year after menarche contribute
strongly to the menstrual irregularity domain score (high weight).
Item 4. Secondary amenorrhea / skipped periods (“Have you ever been told
you have secondary amenorrhea / skipped periods for several months?”)
• Guideline logic: The guideline emphasises that by the third year after
menarche, most cycles approximate adult length, and “well defined
menstrual irregularities are one of the two criteria required for adolescent
PCOS diagnosis.”
• Use in tool: Prolonged absence of menstruation after the expected
maturation period is treated as a high-weight indicator of ovulatory
dysfunction.
2.2 Hyperandrogenism items
Item 5. Hirsutism (“Do you have unwanted hair on your upper lip, chin,
chest, stomach, or back?”)
• Guideline text (Table 4, CR – clinical hyperandrogenism):
o “A comprehensive history and physical examination should be
completed for symptoms and signs of clinical hyperandrogenism,
including acne, female pattern hair loss and hirsutism in adults,
and severe acne and hirsutism in adolescents.”
o “A modified Ferriman-Gallwey (mFG) score of ≥4–6 indicates
hirsutism, acknowledging that self-treatment is common and can
limit clinical assessment.”
• Use in tool:
o Hirsutism signs in androgen-sensitive areas are treated as a core
clinical hyperandrogenism indicator with high weight.
o Self-reported unwanted hair is accepted as meaningful due to
frequent cosmetic removal, in line with practice points recognising
self-assessment validity.
Item 6. Persistent moderate–severe acne (“Do you have acne that has lasted
for several years or is difficult to treat?”)
• Guideline text:
o Mild acne is common in adolescents, but “severe acne during
perimenarcheal years is uncommon and is more likely to indicate
hyperandrogenism.”]
o Consensus recommendations identify severe acne as a sign of
clinical hyperandrogenism in adolescents.
• Use in tool: Persistent, treatment-resistant moderate–severe acne is
scored as clinical hyperandrogenism with medium-to-high weight,
distinguishing it from normal adolescent acne.
Item 7. Scalp hair thinning (“Have you noticed thinning hair on your
scalp?”)
• Guideline text:
o The guideline recommends evaluating “female pattern hair loss” as
part of clinical hyperandrogenism.
o Practice points mention Ludwig/Olsen scales and note that female
pattern hair loss is rare in adolescents but important when present.
• Use in tool: This is a less frequent but significant clinical sign; it receives
moderate weight within the hyperandrogenism domain.
Item 8. Elevated testosterone (“Elevated testosterone (if previously tested)”
)
• Guideline text (Table 4, EBR – biochemical hyperandrogenism):
o “Healthcare professionals should use total and free testosterone to
assess biochemical hyperandrogenism in the diagnosis of PCOS; free
testosterone can be estimated by the calculated free androgen
index.”
o Meta-analysis shows free testosterone and FAI have sensitivity ~80%
and specificity ~86–93% for diagnosing PCOS-related
hyperandrogenism.
• Use in tool: Documented elevation of total/free testosterone or FAI is
treated as a high-weight biochemical hyperandrogenism indicator.
2.3 Weight, metabolic, and insulin resistance items
Item 9. Current height, weight, BMI, and recent weight gain
• Guideline text:
o Practice point: “Healthcare professionals should explain
weight-related health risks to adolescents, while minimizing weight
stigma.”
o Lifestyle PP: “In those who are not overweight, in the adolescent and
key life points, the focus should be on healthy lifestyle and the
prevention of excess weight gain.”
o EBR: “Regardless of age and body mass index, women with PCOS
have an increased risk of impaired fasting glucose, impaired glucose
tolerance, and type 2 diabetes.”
• Use in tool: BMI category and rapid weight gain are treated as metabolic
risk modifiers, not diagnostic criteria. They receive moderate weight and
inform metabolic risk and lifestyle advice.
Item 10. Acanthosis nigricans (“Do you have dark patches around your
neck, underarms, or groin?”)
• Guideline context:
o The guideline emphasises dysglycemia and insulin resistance as key
risks and recommends glycemic assessment in all adolescents with
PCOS.
• Use in tool: Acanthosis nigricans is widely recognised clinically as a
marker of insulin resistance. It is used as a proxy for insulin resistance
and given medium weight within the “Insulin resistance signs” domain,
linked to guideline statements on dysglycemia risk.
Item 11. Skin tags (“Do you have skin tags?”)
• Evidence context: Skin tags are associated with insulin resistance in
general endocrine practice, though they are not listed specifically in the
adolescent PCOS guideline.
• Use in tool: Skin tags are treated as an adjunct sign of possible insulin
resistance, assigned low-to-medium weight and interpreted alongside
acanthosis nigricans and glycemic history.
Item 12. Prior diagnoses (“Have you ever been told you have insulin
resistance / prediabetes / high blood sugar / high cholesterol / high blood
pressure?”)
• Guideline text (other risks, Table 4):
o “Healthcare professionals and women with PCOS should be aware
that, regardless of age and body mass index, women with PCOS have
an increased risk of impaired fasting glucose, impaired glucose
tolerance, and type 2 diabetes.”
o “Glycemic status should be assessed at diagnosis in all adults and
adolescents with PCOS.”
o Practice point: “Healthcare professionals, adults, and adolescents
with PCOS and their first-degree relatives should be aware of the
increased risk of diabetes and the need of regular glycemic
assessment.”
• Use in tool: These items contribute to an “Additional health history”
domain and receive moderate weight as comorbidity indicators, not
diagnostic features.
2.4 Family history items
Items 13–15. “Has your mother ever been diagnosed with PCOS?”, “Do any
of your sisters have PCOS?”, “Does your family have diabetes?”
• Guideline text (other risks, Table 4):
o Practice point: “Healthcare professionals, adults, and adolescents
with PCOS and their first-degree relatives should be aware of the
increased risk of diabetes and the need of regular glycemic
assessment.”
• Use in tool: Family history of PCOS and type 2 diabetes are treated as
genetic/familial risk modifiers with medium weight (~15%), reflecting
strong but non-deterministic familial influence.
2.5 Lifestyle and psychosocial items
Items 16–19. “Exercise frequency”, “Sleep duration”, “Unhealthy diet”,
“Physical inactivity”
• Guideline text (Lifestyle management, Table 4):
o EBR: “Lifestyle interventions (exercise alone or multicomponent diet
combined with exercise and behavioral strategies) should be
recommended for all women with PCOS, for improving metabolic
health including central adiposity and lipid profile.”
o CR: “Adolescents should aim for at least 60 min of moderate to
vigorous intensity physical activity per day including activities that
strengthen muscle and bone at least three times a week.”
o EBR: No specific diet composition or exercise type is superior for
PCOS outcomes.
• Use in tool: Lifestyle factors are low-weight modifiers that inform
metabolic risk and personalised recommendations, rather than PCOS
probability.
Items 20–24. “Poor sleep”, “Stress”, “Mood swings”, “Depression”,
“Anxiety”
• Guideline text (Psychological features, Table 4):
o EBR: “Healthcare professionals should be aware of the high
prevalence of moderate to severe depressive symptoms and
depression in adults and adolescents with PCOS and should screen
for depression.”
o EBR: Similar recommendation for anxiety in adults with PCOS.
• Use in tool:
o Depression and anxiety questions are included outside the main
PCOS risk score, used to trigger supportive messages and advice for
mental-health evaluation.
o They receive minimal or no weight in PCOS risk calculation due to
nonspecificity, consistent with guideline emphasis on psychological
comorbidity rather than diagnostic status.
2.6 Items explicitly excluded or down-weighted
Items 25–26. Pelvic ultrasound (PCOM) and AMH
• Guideline text (Investigations not recommended, Table 4):
o Practice point: “There are no definitive criteria to define polycystic
ovary morphology on ultrasound in adolescents; hence, it is not
recommended in adolescents.”
o EBR: “AMH should not yet be used in adolescents.”
• Use in tool:
o Ultrasound PCOM and AMH results are not used as risk
contributors in adolescents.
o If provided, the tool explains that these parameters are not
recommended for adolescent PCOS diagnosis to avoid overdiagnosis.
3. Scoring algorithm and risk categories
3.1 Domains and maximum points (total 100)
Each questionnaire item contributes to one of six domains:
• Menstrual irregularities – 30 points
• Hyperandrogenism – 30 points
• Family history – 15 points
• Insulin resistance signs – 10 points
• BMI & recent weight change – 10 points
• Lifestyle factors – 5 points
Rationale: Menstrual irregularities and hyperandrogenism are the two diagnostic
criteria in adolescents and therefore carry 60% of the total score. Family,
metabolic, and lifestyle factors modify risk but do not define PCOS, so they have
lower weights.
3.2 Within-domain scoring (examples)
Menstrual irregularities (0–30)
• No irregularity beyond physiologic range for gynecologic age → 0.
• Irregular cycles within expected puberty maturation (<3 years
post-menarche) but improving → ~10.
• Persistent irregular cycles ≥3 years post-menarche (<21 or >35 days, <8
cycles/year) → ~20–25.
• Any cycle >90 days ≥1 year post-menarche → additional points up to 30.
Hyperandrogenism (0–30)
• Mild acne only, no hirsutism, no scalp hair loss → 0–5.
• Hirsutism symptoms or persistent moderate–severe acne → ~15–20.
• Elevated total/free testosterone or FAI plus clinical signs → ~25–30.
• Other domains follow the point ranges previously outlined (family history
0–15, insulin resistance 0–10, BMI/weight change 0–10, lifestyle 0–5),
based on presence and severity of relevant factors.
3.3 Risk category definitions
Low risk of PCOS
• Total score <30 and
• Menstrual domain <15 and hyperandrogenism domain <15.
Interpretation: No strong guideline-defined diagnostic features; symptoms likely
reflect normal pubertal changes or nonspecific factors.
Moderate risk / “at risk” of PCOS
• EITHER
o Menstrual domain ≥20 and hyperandrogenism domain <15,
o OR hyperandrogenism domain ≥20 and menstrual domain <15.
Interpretation: Exactly one major diagnostic feature is clearly present, matching
the guideline category “adolescents who have only one of the two features
required for adolescent PCOS diagnosis should be considered ‘at risk’.”
High risk / likely PCOS pattern
• Menstrual domain ≥20 and hyperandrogenism domain ≥20.
• Total score typically ≥60.
Interpretation: The pattern matches adolescent diagnostic criteria (irregular
cycles plus hyperandrogenism) from Peña et al. 2025. In the tool, this is labelled
“high risk” rather than a formal diagnosis, and users are advised to seek clinical
evaluation to exclude mimicking conditions.
4. Distinction between screening and diagnosis
The AI-assisted tool is explicitly designed as a screening and risk estimation
system, not a diagnostic device. To maintain this distinction:
• The tool uses language such as “low risk”, “moderate risk / at risk”, and
“high risk / likely PCOS pattern” rather than “diagnosed PCOS”.
• It never uses ultrasound PCOM or AMH to increase adolescent PCOS
probability, in line with the recommendation that both are not suitable for
adolescent diagnosis.
• For high-risk users, the tool explains that their pattern is consistent with
guideline diagnostic criteria but emphasises the need for clinical
assessment, laboratory testing where appropriate, and exclusion of
conditions that mimic PCOS.
5. Example guideline-anchored wording for protocol
Include the following (adapted) statements in study documents:
• “Our AI-assisted screening tool for adolescent PCOS is modelled after Peña
et al. (2025), which states that adolescent diagnosis is based on the
presence of both irregular menstrual cycles (defined by years
post-menarche) and clinical/biochemical hyperandrogenism following
exclusion of other disorders that mimic PCOS.”
• “Participants are classified as low, moderate (‘at risk’), or high risk
according to whether they exhibit none, one, or both of these features,
consistent with the guideline recommendation that adolescents with only
one feature should be considered ‘at risk’ and require longitudinal
follow-up.”
• “Pelvic ultrasound and AMH are intentionally excluded from the
adolescent risk calculation, in line with guideline practice points stating
that PCOM on ultrasound and AMH should not be used for diagnosis
during adolescence due to poor specificity and dynamic ovarian
morphology during puberty.”
